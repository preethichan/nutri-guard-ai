# High Blood Triglycerides (NHLBI, NIH)

## What are triglycerides?
Triglycerides are a type of fat (lipid) that circulates in the blood. They are the most common type of fat in the body. Triglycerides come from foods, especially butter, oils, and other dietary fats, and also from extra calories the body does not need right away — unused calories are stored as triglycerides in fat cells and released for energy later. The body needs some triglycerides for good health, but high levels in the blood can raise the risk of heart disease and stroke.

High blood triglycerides are a type of lipid disorder. This condition can occur on its own, alongside other lipid disorders such as high blood cholesterol or low HDL cholesterol, or as part of metabolic syndrome. High blood triglyceride levels are very common — up to one in four adults are affected.

## What are healthy blood triglyceride levels?
Triglyceride levels usually fall into the following categories (fasting):

- **Healthy:** Below 150 mg/dL for adults; lower than 90 mg/dL for children and teens (ages 10–19)
- **Borderline high:** 150–199 mg/dL
- **High:** 200–499 mg/dL
- **Very high:** Above 500 mg/dL

A healthcare provider may diagnose high blood triglycerides if fasting blood triglyceride levels are regularly 150 mg/dL or higher.

## Symptoms and risks
High blood triglycerides usually do not cause any symptoms. If untreated or uncontrolled, they can increase the risk of serious complications:

- Borderline-high and high triglycerides (non-fasting levels that stay above 175 mg/dL for a long time) can raise the risk for heart complications.
- Very high triglycerides (>500 mg/dL) can raise the risk of acute pancreatitis (sudden swelling of the pancreas), lipemia retinalis (changes in the eye's blood vessels), and damaged skin on the back, chest, arms, and legs.
- Extremely high triglycerides (>1,500 mg/dL) may cause multifactorial chylomicronemia syndrome, with symptoms including short-term memory loss, swelling of the liver and spleen, stomach pain, and skin flushing with alcohol use.

## Causes and risk factors
Health conditions that may increase blood triglyceride levels include:
- Kidney disease
- Diabetes
- Liver disease
- Overweight and obesity
- Thyroid disease

Lifestyle factors that can raise triglycerides:
- Not getting enough exercise
- Eating foods high in fat and sugar
- Drinking too much alcohol

Some medicines can raise triglyceride levels, including certain treatments for breast cancer, HIV, and (less often, usually mildly) high blood pressure.

Populations with potentially higher risk:
- People living with HIV
- People with an inherited lipid metabolism disorder that prevents the body from breaking down lipids
- People of South Asian ancestry (e.g., Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka) living in the United States

> **Scope note:** Several of these causes (kidney disease, diabetes, liver disease, thyroid disease, genetic lipid disorders) require clinical diagnosis and medical management — they are **not** something a dietary/nutrition assistant should attempt to diagnose or treat. Dietary guidance should be framed as general lifestyle support, with a recommendation to consult a healthcare provider for diagnosis or when symptoms/risk factors above are present.

## Diagnosis
A lipoprotein profile (including a triglycerides test) is typically recommended every 5 years for healthy adults; more frequent testing may be advised for older adults, people with diabetes, or those with a family history of high cholesterol or other heart disease risk factors. Fasting for several hours is often required before testing. Apolipoprotein B testing may be used when triglyceride levels are borderline high. Genetic testing may be considered for people with pancreatitis or very high triglycerides without diabetes/obesity, or a close blood relative with very high triglycerides or pancreatitis.

## Lifestyle changes
If diagnosed with high blood triglycerides, a healthcare provider may recommend:
- Choosing heart-healthy foods
- Getting regular physical activity
- Aiming for a healthy weight
- Managing stress
- Quitting smoking
- Getting enough good-quality sleep
- Limiting alcohol (providers may recommend stopping alcohol entirely if triglycerides are very high)

## Medicines
A healthcare provider may prescribe:
- **Fibrates** — lower triglycerides in the blood; may also lower cholesterol
- **Concentrated prescription omega-3 fatty acids** — approved for treating triglyceride levels ≥150 mg/dL; these are manufactured differently than over-the-counter omega-3 dietary supplements, which are **not** FDA-approved to treat high triglycerides
- **Statins** — most common medicine for high cholesterol, can also lower triglycerides
- **Ezetimibe** — another cholesterol-lowering medicine that can help remove triglycerides from the blood when paired with a healthy diet

More than one medicine may be needed for very high triglyceride levels.

> **Scope note:** Medication decisions are medical/clinical, not dietary advice. A nutrition assistant should not recommend starting, stopping, or dosing any medication (including over-the-counter omega-3 supplements framed as a treatment) — this should be deferred to a healthcare provider.
