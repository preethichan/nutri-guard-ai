# Secondary Medical Causes & When to Escalate to a Healthcare Provider

This document consolidates medical/clinical causes of elevated triglycerides mentioned across NHLBI, MedlinePlus, and AHA sources. It exists specifically to define the **scope boundary** for a dietary/nutrition assistant: these are conditions that require clinical diagnosis and treatment, not dietary advice alone.

## Medical conditions that can cause or worsen high triglycerides
(Per NHLBI, MedlinePlus, and AHA — see `01_triglyceride_basics_nhlbi.md`, `02_triglyceride_levels_medlineplus.md`, `06_alcohol_and_refined_carbs_aha.md`)
- Diabetes, especially poorly controlled type 2 diabetes
- Kidney disease (including nephrotic syndrome)
- Liver disease
- Hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid)
- Genetic / inherited lipid metabolism disorders (e.g., familial hypertriglyceridemia)
- Chronic kidney disease
- Systemic inflammatory diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus)
- Pregnancy (especially third trimester)
- Certain medications (e.g., some breast cancer treatments, HIV medications, thiazide diuretics, retinoids, some blood pressure medicines)

AHA clinical guidance explicitly states that **before any triglyceride-lowering therapy is initiated, reversible secondary causes (such as hypothyroidism or poorly controlled type 2 diabetes) should be identified and treated** by a clinician — underscoring that dietary changes alone are not a substitute for medical evaluation when a secondary cause may be present.

## Red-flag symptoms warranting prompt medical attention
Per NHLBI, very high or extremely high triglyceride levels can cause:
- Acute pancreatitis (sudden pancreas swelling) — a medical emergency
- Lipemia retinalis (visible changes in eye blood vessels)
- Skin changes/damage on the back, chest, arms, and legs
- At extremely high levels (>1,500 mg/dL): short-term memory loss, swelling of the liver and spleen, stomach pain, and skin flushing with alcohol use

## Guidance for a dietary assistant (derived scope boundary)
Based on the above sources, a nutrition/dietary chatbot should:
1. **Provide general, source-grounded dietary information** (e.g., reducing added sugar/alcohol, choosing unsaturated fats, increasing fiber and omega-3-rich foods) as covered in this knowledge base.
2. **Not diagnose** a user's condition, underlying cause, or specific triglyceride level/category based on user-reported symptoms.
3. **Not recommend starting, stopping, or adjusting medications or supplement dosages** as a treatment — only general dietary/food-source information (e.g., "fatty fish is a dietary source of omega-3s") should be given; dosing/treatment questions should be redirected to a healthcare provider.
4. **Escalate / redirect to a healthcare provider** when a user mentions: very high reported triglyceride levels, symptoms consistent with pancreatitis (e.g., severe abdominal pain), pregnancy, known diabetes/kidney/liver/thyroid disease, or a request for medical diagnosis.
5. **Avoid fabricating levels, recipes, or claims** not grounded in the retrieved source documents — especially around lab values, supplement dosing, and medication interactions, where small inaccuracies can carry real health risk.
