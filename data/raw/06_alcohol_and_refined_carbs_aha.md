# Alcohol, Refined Carbohydrates, and Triglycerides (American Heart Association)

## Alcohol's effect on triglycerides
Increased alcohol intake can aggravate hypertriglyceridemia (elevated blood triglycerides). This is a well-established dietary contributor alongside excess sugar intake and, in cases of severe/very high triglycerides, excess total dietary fat.

AHA alcohol intake guidance (from AHA Dietary Guidelines):
> "If alcoholic beverages are consumed, they should be limited to the equivalent of 2 drinks (30 g ethanol) per day for men and 1 drink per day for women." Alcohol has a higher caloric density than protein and carbohydrate and is a source of additional "empty" calories.

More recent AHA guidance (2021 Dietary Guidance to Improve Cardiovascular Health) takes a more cautious stance:
> "If you do not drink alcohol, do not start. If you choose to drink alcohol, limit intake." The statement notes uncertainty about alcohol's net health effects, given its association with injuries, violence, digestive diseases, infectious diseases, pregnancy complications, and cancer. The 2020–2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans recommend no more than 1 drink per day for women and 2 drinks per day for men, for those who choose to drink.

For people with very high triglycerides, NHLBI notes a healthcare provider may recommend **stopping alcohol use entirely**.

## Refined carbohydrates and sugar
When carbohydrates replace dietary fat, they can elevate plasma triglyceride levels and lower HDL ("good") cholesterol. Elevations in fasting plasma triglycerides (principally VLDL) are a consistent feature of diets high (≥20% of energy) in sucrose, glucose, and fructose. This effect is most pronounced in the context of insulin resistance and glucose intolerance.

Excess body weight, reduced physical activity, and increased intake of sugar and refined carbohydrates are all associated with the combination of low HDL cholesterol and elevated triglycerides. For individuals with this combination, AHA guidance points to:
- Weight reduction
- Increased physical activity
- Reduced carbohydrate intake (specifically refined/added sugars)

For severe hypertriglyceridemia associated with chylomicronemia, restriction of dietary fat (not just sugar/alcohol) is indicated, and increased intake of omega-3 fatty acids may be beneficial.

## Common contributing factors (AHA summary table, from 2017 Science Advisory on Omega-3s and Hypertriglyceridemia)
Factors that can contribute to elevated triglycerides include:
- Overweight or obesity
- Insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome
- Diabetes mellitus, especially with poor glycemic control
- Alcohol consumption, especially in excess
- Excess sugar intake, especially from food processing
- High saturated fat intake
- Hypothyroidism
- Chronic kidney disease
- Nephrotic syndrome
- Sedentary lifestyle
- Pregnancy (especially third trimester)
- Certain medications

> **Scope note:** Before recommending triglyceride-lowering dietary changes, clinical guidance calls for first identifying and treating reversible secondary causes (e.g., hypothyroidism, poorly controlled type 2 diabetes) and reviewing medications that may be contributing — all of which require a healthcare provider, not a dietary assistant.
