# Added / Free Sugars and Triglycerides

## WHO Guideline: Sugars Intake for Adults and Children (2015)
WHO recommends a reduced intake of free sugars throughout the life course:
- In both adults and children, WHO recommends reducing the intake of **free sugars to less than 10% of total energy intake** (strong recommendation).
- WHO suggests a further reduction of free sugars intake to **below 5% of total energy intake** for additional health benefits (conditional recommendation).

**Free sugars** are defined as all sugars added to foods or drinks by the manufacturer, cook, or consumer, as well as sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, fruit juices, and fruit juice concentrates.

For a person consuming about 2,000 calories per day, less than 10% of energy from free sugars is equivalent to about 50 g (around 12 level teaspoons) per day.

Consuming free sugars increases the risk of dental caries. Excess calories from foods and drinks high in free sugars also contribute to unhealthy weight gain. Evidence shows that free sugars influence blood pressure and serum lipids, and a reduction in free sugar intake reduces cardiovascular disease risk factors.

WHO suggests reducing sugar intake by:
- Limiting consumption of foods/drinks high in sugar (sugary snacks, candies, sugar-sweetened beverages, soft drinks, fruit/vegetable juices and drinks, flavored water, energy/sports drinks, ready-to-drink tea/coffee, flavored milk drinks)
- Eating fresh fruit and raw vegetables as snacks instead of sugary snacks

*Source: WHO. Guideline: Sugars intake for adults and children. Geneva: World Health Organization; 2015.*

## USDA / HHS Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2020-2025 (Added Sugars)
The Dietary Guidelines for Americans set the following limit:
- **Added sugars:** Less than 10 percent of calories per day, starting at age 2. Foods and beverages with added sugars should be avoided for children younger than age 2.

This recommendation (retained from the 2015–2020 edition) is based on food pattern modeling: most Americans have less than 8% of calories available for added sugars within a nutrient-dense healthy dietary pattern. For people who need higher calorie intakes, an upper limit of 10% of calories from added sugars may be consumed while still meeting food group recommendations — but this requires careful planning, since a healthy dietary pattern doesn't leave much room for extra added sugars alongside saturated fat and sodium limits.

*Source: Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2020-2025, USDA/HHS.*

## Relevance to triglycerides
Both WHO and AHA evidence links excess intake of free/added sugars (particularly fructose and sucrose) to elevated fasting triglycerides and reduced HDL ("good") cholesterol, especially in the context of insulin resistance. Limiting sugar-sweetened beverages and refined/added sugars is one of the most consistently recommended dietary strategies for managing elevated triglycerides.
