# Omega-3 Fatty Acids (NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, Consumer Fact Sheet)

## What are omega-3 fatty acids?
Omega-3 fatty acids are found in foods such as fish and flaxseed, and in dietary supplements such as fish oil. The three main types are:
- **ALA** (alpha-linolenic acid) — found mainly in plant oils (flaxseed, soybean, canola oils); an essential fatty acid the body cannot make, so it must come from food
- **EPA** (eicosapentaenoic acid) and **DHA** (docosahexaenoic acid) — found in fish and other seafood

The body can convert some ALA into EPA and then DHA, but only in small amounts — so getting EPA and DHA directly from food (or supplements) is the practical way to raise levels of these omega-3s.

## How much ALA do I need? (Recommended Dietary Allowances)
| Life Stage | Recommended Amount of ALA |
|---|---|
| Birth to 12 months* | 0.5 g |
| Children 1–3 years | 0.7 g |
| Children 4–8 years | 0.9 g |
| Boys 9–13 years | 1.2 g |
| Girls 9–13 years | 1.0 g |
| Teen boys 14–18 years | 1.6 g |
| Teen girls 14–18 years | 1.1 g |
| Men | 1.6 g |
| Women | 1.1 g |
| Pregnant teens and women | 1.4 g |
| Breastfeeding teens and women | 1.3 g |

*As total omega-3s for infants; all other values are for ALA alone. No established recommended amounts exist for EPA/DHA specifically — most people in the U.S. get enough ALA from food and small amounts of EPA/DHA.

## Food sources
- Fish and other seafood — especially cold-water fatty fish: salmon, mackerel, tuna, herring, sardines
- Nuts and seeds — flaxseed, chia seeds, walnuts
- Plant oils — flaxseed oil, soybean oil, canola oil
- Fortified foods — certain brands of eggs, yogurt, juices, milk, soy beverages, infant formulas

## Supplements
Omega-3 dietary supplements include fish oil, krill oil, cod liver oil, and algal oil (a vegetarian source from algae), providing a wide range of doses and forms.

## Omega-3s and cardiovascular health (relevant to triglycerides)
Many studies show that eating fatty fish/seafood as part of a healthy eating pattern helps protect heart health. **Getting more EPA and DHA from foods or dietary supplements lowers triglyceride levels.**

- The American Heart Association (AHA) recommends **1–2 servings of seafood per week** to reduce cardiovascular risk, especially when consumed in place of less healthy foods.
- For people with heart disease, the AHA recommends about **1 g/day of EPA plus DHA**, preferably from oily fish; supplements are an option under a health care provider's guidance.
- The AHA does **not** recommend omega-3 supplements for people without a high cardiovascular risk.

## Safety
- The U.S. FDA recommends consuming **no more than 5 g/day of EPA and DHA combined from dietary supplements**.
- Side effects are usually mild: unpleasant taste, bad breath, heartburn, nausea, stomach discomfort, diarrhea, headache, smelly sweat.
- **Drug interaction:** High doses of omega-3s may cause bleeding problems when combined with warfarin (Coumadin) or other anticoagulant medicines — consult a healthcare provider about interactions with medications.

## General guidance
People should get most nutrients from food and beverages per the Dietary Guidelines for Americans; fortified foods and supplements are useful when dietary needs can't otherwise be met (e.g., during pregnancy).

> **Scope note:** Distinguish between (a) general dietary guidance — eating more fatty fish/omega-3-rich foods — which is appropriate for a nutrition assistant to discuss, and (b) supplement dosing recommendations or substituting supplements for prescribed triglyceride-lowering medication, which should be deferred to a healthcare provider. Also note NHLBI's distinction: **prescription** concentrated omega-3s (FDA-approved for triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL) are different from over-the-counter omega-3 supplements, which are not FDA-approved to treat high triglycerides.
