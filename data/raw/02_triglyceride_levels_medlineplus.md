# Triglycerides (MedlinePlus, NIH)

## What are triglycerides?
Triglycerides are a type of fat — the most common type of fat in the body. They come from foods, especially butter, oils, and other dietary fats. Triglycerides also come from extra calories: calories eaten but not needed right away are converted into triglycerides and stored in fat cells. When the body needs energy, it releases the triglycerides; VLDL cholesterol particles carry the triglycerides to body tissues. Having a high triglyceride level can raise the risk of heart disease, such as coronary artery disease.

## What causes high triglycerides?
Factors that can raise triglyceride levels include:
- Regularly eating more calories than you burn off, especially if you eat a lot of sugar
- Being overweight or having obesity
- Cigarette smoking
- Excessive alcohol use
- Certain medicines
- Some genetic disorders
- Thyroid diseases
- Poorly controlled type 2 diabetes
- Liver or kidney diseases

## How are high triglycerides diagnosed?
A blood test measures triglycerides along with cholesterol, in milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL):

| Category | Triglyceride Level |
|---|---|
| Normal | Less than 150 mg/dL |
| Borderline high | 150 to 199 mg/dL |
| High | 200 to 499 mg/dL |
| Very high | 500 mg/dL and above |

Levels above 150 mg/dL may raise the risk for heart disease. A triglyceride level of 150 mg/dL or higher is also a risk factor for metabolic syndrome.

## What are the treatments for high triglycerides?
Triglyceride levels may be lowered with lifestyle changes:
- Controlling weight
- Regular physical activity
- Not smoking
- Limiting sugar and refined foods
- Limiting alcohol
- Switching from saturated fats to healthier fats

Some people will also need cholesterol-lowering medicines to lower their triglycerides.

> **Scope note:** Genetic disorders, thyroid disease, diabetes, and liver/kidney disease listed as causes are medical conditions requiring clinical diagnosis and treatment — outside the scope of dietary guidance alone. A nutrition assistant should encourage consulting a healthcare provider rather than attempting to diagnose the underlying cause of elevated triglycerides.
